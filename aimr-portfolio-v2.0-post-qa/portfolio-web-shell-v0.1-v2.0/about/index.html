About

Andrew Nixon · Independent researcher

I am an independent researcher developing the Human–AI Developmental Research Program (HAIDR), working toward a design science of human–AI developmental ecologies. HAIDR is a candidate integrative research architecture connecting nested ecologies, participation history, and sustained capability across human, coupled, collective, and institutional levels. My work brings together questions from human development, philosophy, psychology, systems thinking, human–AI interaction, education, and embodied practice.

My intellectual trajectory began well before generative AI. Early work in sustainability, environmental ethics, philosophy, and systems-oriented approaches to complex problems led me repeatedly toward questions about development: how persons and systems become more differentiated and integrated, how coherence can emerge without becoming rigid, and how forms of participation shape what becomes possible over time. Across these domains, I became increasingly interested not only in what people think, but in the conditions under which capacities, perspectives, and forms of agency develop. Sustainability and environmental philosophy also established a lasting concern with the ways human development is embedded within relationships to social, material, and more-than-human worlds.

That concern has also been practical rather than exclusively academic. Years of work in teaching, music, bodywork, and contemplative practice have involved different versions of the same problem: how attention is cultivated, how skill becomes increasingly internalized, how perception and interpretation develop through repeated engagement, and how responsive interaction can support another person without replacing their own agency. Teaching has made learning and developmental scaffolding concrete; music has foregrounded attention, timing, interpretation, and coordinated participation; and a 20-year career in bodywork, together with a longstanding mindfulness and contemplative practice, has kept questions of cognition connected to embodied experience, regulation, interpersonal responsiveness, and forms of knowing that precede or exceed explicit conceptualization.

My relationship with the more-than-human natural world has provided another persistent counterpoint to primarily linguistic and technological forms of reflection. Time in nature has shaped my understanding of attention, embodiment, relationality, and the importance of maintaining contact with conditions that are not organized around human intention or conversational responsiveness. This has become increasingly relevant to my AI research: developmental environments should be evaluated not only by what mediation makes possible, but by what forms of embodied, interpersonal, material, and ecological participation they preserve, deepen, redirect, or displace.

The emergence of persistent generative-AI systems brought these longstanding interests into a new empirical domain. Sustained human–AI interaction can now support reflection, inquiry, planning, interpretation, and problem-solving recursively across time. But this creates a deeper scientific question than whether AI improves performance: does sustained participation with these systems change the human participant, through what mechanisms, under what conditions, and with what consequences for independent capacity and agency? AI-Mediated Reflection (AIMR) is now a bounded research site within HAIDR, with Study 0 as its current empirical anchor.

These apparently different domains therefore converge on a persistent concern with development, participation, and coherence: how environments organize attention and action; how repeated participation alters capacities; how support can enable rather than displace agency; and how developmental change remains corrigible through contact with other people, evidence, consequences, embodied experience, and the more-than-human world.

I am now seeking doctoral training that will allow HAIDR to develop through rigorous philosophical and conceptual inquiry in sustained contact with empirical research, combining longitudinal and experimental approaches with the conceptual work needed to understand human development in increasingly persistent human–AI environments. My aim is to develop, test, revise, and, where necessary, transform the framework through sustained empirical research while remaining attentive to the embodied, relational, and ecological dimensions of human development that technological mediation may both support and disrupt.
