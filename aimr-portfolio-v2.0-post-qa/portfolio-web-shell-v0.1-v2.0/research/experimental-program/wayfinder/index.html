Wayfinder

Adaptive developmental environments · AI-mediated learning

Can adaptive challenge, productive friction, and progressively calibrated scaffolding create conditions in which human capacities develop through repeated enactment, transfer, and increasing agency?

Candidate experimental and design program

Wayfinder would investigate AI-mediated environments that dynamically organize developmental challenges around the evolving capacities of the participant. Rather than gamifying performance through points, badges, or external rewards, the system would treat development itself as the progression architecture: increasingly differentiated challenges, productive friction, adaptive scaffolding, repeated enactment, scaffold reduction, and opportunities for transfer into new contexts.

The central design problem is to create environments that optimize neither ease nor immediate task performance, but developmentally productive participation.

AI makes it possible to alter challenge complexity, feedback, timing, assistance, representation, pacing, and access to scaffolds in response to a participant’s emerging capacities. Wayfinder would experimentally investigate which configurations keep cognitive operations within the participant’s realizable range while requiring sufficient effort, discrimination, correction, and self-initiation for those operations to become increasingly their own.

A candidate developmental cycle is:

adaptive challenge → productive friction → scaffolded enactment → feedback and recognition → repeated enactment → progressive scaffold reduction → transfer → increasing agency

The crucial outcome is therefore not whether participants advance through the environment. It is whether capacities cultivated within it become portable beyond it.

This creates experimental contrasts between environments optimized for immediate success and environments optimized for development. Two participants could ultimately achieve comparable assisted performance while differing substantially in independent transfer, spontaneous initiation, adaptability, and scaffold dependence.

Recent evidence makes this distinction particularly timely. A 2026 experiment with 180 children found stronger narrative performance during iterative AI-supported practice but no corresponding between-condition advantage when children transferred the skill without AI support. Another 2026 study experimentally manipulated when learners could access GenAI and found benefits from strategically timed rather than unrestricted access. These results do not establish Wayfinder’s hypotheses, but they demonstrate that scaffolding structure and unsupported transfer are separable empirical questions.
