Human–AI Developmental Research

How do sustained forms of human–AI participation shape what people, organizations, and societies become capable of?

5-minute overview · canonical portfolio version · October 2026

The problem: performance is not development

Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming part of the environments through which people think, learn, research, decide, coordinate, create knowledge, and exercise agency. As these relationships become persistent, the relevant question is no longer only what AI can help us do now, but how repeated participation with AI changes what becomes possible later.

AI may improve performance while leaving independent human competence largely unchanged. It may also scaffold new forms of judgment, accelerate learning, reorganize agency toward higher-order capacities, or support productive specialization between human and artificial systems. Conversely, repeated delegation may weaken capacities through disuse, remove opportunities through which expertise would otherwise develop, or create dependencies that remain invisible while support is available.

These futures can look remarkably similar if we measure only immediate task performance.

The central problem for Human–AI Developmental Research (HAIDR) is therefore longitudinal:

How does sustained participation in differently configured human–AI environments shape human capacities, coupled capabilities, and human–AI pathways over time?

The question is deliberately neutral about whether those changes are beneficial. Longitudinal change may involve capacity formation, reorganization, attenuation, displacement, specialization, or changing distributions of agency between humans and artificial systems.

HAIDR’s integrative move

Human–AI research already contains substantial work on learning, cognitive offloading, function allocation, interaction design, agency, organizational change, collective intelligence, and longitudinal effects. HAIDR does not begin from the claim that these dimensions have been overlooked.

Its distinguishing move is to treat them as parts of a common developmental mapping problem:

How do nested conditions, distributions of human and artificial activity, and histories of repeated participation interact to produce different capability trajectories across people, coupled systems, collectives, and institutions?

Suppose a human–AI configuration improves performance. What evidence would justify concluding that the human developed a new capacity, that the coupled system became more capable, that an organization preserved expertise rather than merely increasing throughput, or that a collective or institution gained coordination or resilience rather than hidden dependence?

HAIDR aims to make these relations—and the inferential gaps between them—explicit. Its contribution depends on whether a common developmental architecture improves our ability to distinguish, explain, measure, and eventually design the different longitudinal consequences that existing human–AI research is beginning to reveal.

From developmental ecology to enacted participation

HAIDR treats the developmental ecology as its principal theoretical and design object: the organized conditions through which recurrent human–AI participation is structured and developmental consequences become more or less likely.

Within that ecology, a local configuration includes proximal, manipulable features such as the AI system, memory and retrieval architecture, interface, tools, scaffolding, continuity, and the capacities the human brings to participation.

A participation protocol specifies how participation is intended to occur: initiative, authority, feedback, assistance, turn structure, and task organization.

But intended participation is not necessarily enacted participation.

HAIDR therefore distinguishes the protocol from the enacted participation topology: the actual relational organization of consequential activity within the system.

Who actually does what?

Who formulates the problem? Who retrieves information? Who generates possibilities? Who interprets, judges, monitors, corrects, decides, and acts?

The same AI model can therefore participate in very different developmental arrangements. An occasional critic, a persistent collaborator, a delegated agent, and an embedded organizational system may distribute opportunities for practice, judgment, correction, and learning quite differently.

These relations are recursive. Changed human capacities alter later participation; repeated participation may change workflows, roles, expectations, and eventually institutional arrangements, which in turn reshape the developmental ecology.

Change may begin within particular interactions, stabilize or reorganize across repeated participation, and eventually become a durable shift in repertoire, agency, expertise, dependence, identity, role, or the range of strategies a person or system can realistically enact.

What capability actually changed?

Consider two workflows both described as “human review.” In one, a person makes an independent judgment before seeing the AI’s recommendation and then resolves discrepancies. In another, the AI performs the substantive analysis and the person approves the result unless something appears wrong.

Administratively, both contain a human “in the loop.” Developmentally, they may be very different. They provide different opportunities to formulate judgments, notice anomalies, diagnose errors, revise understanding, and maintain the capacity to intervene when the system fails.

What capability changed, where does it now reside, what does it depend upon, and what happens when conditions change?

The answer should not automatically privilege unaided human performance. External support can constitute genuine capability, and delegating some operations may permit more valuable supervisory, integrative, or strategic capacities to develop.

The scientific task is to discriminate among outcomes such as durable human capacity formation, productive complementary specialization, higher-order reorganization of agency, stable dependence, and capacity displacement.

How I am beginning to test it

Study 0 is HAIDR’s current empirical anchor: an intensive longitudinal case of sustained human–AI reflective participation.

Its retrospective phase reconstructs participation patterns, changing capacity distributions, and candidate developmental processes from a large longitudinal archive. External digital traces can corroborate, qualify, or contradict archive-based interpretations, while not by themselves establishing independent development or causality.

Its prospective component is designed to use repeated research-reasoning probes, delay, transfer, perturbation, and scaffold reduction or withdrawal to distinguish what has become independently available to the human; what remains dependent on continued human–AI coupling; and what exists primarily as an emergent capability of the coupled system.

Study 0 is a discovery prototype and hypothesis generator, not evidence sufficient to validate HAIDR as a general framework.

The broader methodological program distinguishes discovery → causal discrimination → configuration optimization.

Discovery identifies candidate regularities, mechanisms, and consequential dimensions of participation. Stronger studies can then manipulate or compare features of the developmental ecology, local configuration, or protocol; verify the topology actually enacted; and test what persists, transfers, reorganizes, or becomes displaced when conditions change.

Optimization compares configurations against specified developmental objectives and constraints; successful optimization does not itself establish mechanism. The aim is ultimately to compare configurations of participation, not simply AI use with non-use.

Toward a design science

The longer-term ambition is not to discover one universally ideal human–AI relationship. It is to build conditional design knowledge:

Which configurations of human–AI participation tend to produce which developmental pathways, for whom, under what conditions, across which levels, timescales, and tradeoffs?

A mature design science would distinguish systems that merely improve present performance from those that cultivate durable human capacities, productive complementary specialization, higher-order agency, collective expertise, or institutional resilience—and from those that produce dependence or displacement hidden until circumstances change.

Human–AI systems are especially important because they are both historically consequential and unusually tractable developmental environments: models, memory, delegation, continuity, interfaces, scaffolds, and participation protocols can often be deliberately reconfigured and compared.

The empirical work begins with bounded human–AI systems. The wider horizon is:

Under what conditions does increasing artificial intelligence also increase humanity’s capacity to understand, govern, and respond wisely to the conditions it is creating?
