5-Minute HAIDR Overview

Human–AI Developmental Research

From supported performance to durable human development

A concise orientation to the research program

1. The research problem

Generative AI is rapidly changing what people can accomplish while working with intelligent systems. Human–AI interaction research has accordingly developed strong literatures on augmented performance, human–AI collaboration, cognitive offloading, trust and reliance, AI-supported learning, tutoring, scaffolding, and increasingly the distribution of cognitive work between human and machine.

A developmental problem cuts across these literatures:

Improved performance by a human–AI system does not establish that the human participant has developed.

A person may reason better, write better, solve a harder problem, or operate at a higher level while AI support is present. But the increased capability may reside principally in the coupled system. The developmental question is what happens to the human across repeated participation: what capacities are exercised or displaced, what becomes stabilized, what transfers, and what remains available when the configuration changes or the scaffold is removed.

This problem is increasingly visible in adjacent research. Studies now distinguish collaborative performance from skill development and investigate cognitive offloading, metacognitive calibration, scaffolding, and human–AI co-regulation. Experimental findings suggest that developmental consequences depend on how assistance is configured: some forms improve immediate performance while weakening subsequent independent performance or skill development, whereas others support later independent performance. Reviews likewise call for stronger longitudinal and process-sensitive research.

Beyond these empirical questions, a broader normative horizon is also coming into view. Across adjacent work on AI, learning, alignment, sustainability, and governance, researchers are increasingly asking not only what AI enables, but what forms of human and ecological flourishing it should support, how it affects agency and collective capacity, and whether it helps societies respond to — or compounds — the interconnected systemic crises of the polycrisis.

For HAIDR, this raises a further question: whether the developmental consequences of human–AI participation strengthen or erode the human capacities required to sustain viable collective futures.

HAIDR begins where these concerns converge.

The proposed gap is therefore not that development, learning, scaffolding, or cognitive offloading are absent from AI research. What remains underdeveloped is an integrated longitudinal account of how differently configured human–AI environments organize repeated participation, how that participation alters human capacities and developmental possibilities over time, and how those changes can be distinguished from the capability of the coupled human–AI system.

2. The research move: human–AI developmental ecologies

The Human–AI Developmental Research Program (HAIDR) is being developed to address that integrative gap. It organizes longitudinal developmental research around configurable ecologies and durable human-capacity change. More specifically, it treats sustained human–AI participation as occurring within a developmental ecology.

The term requires some care. “Ecological” has several established meanings across developmental science. Bronfenbrenner’s ecological and later bioecological approaches locate development within interacting person, process, context, and time relations, with proximal processes becoming central to the mature model. Other ecological-relational and dynamic-systems traditions emphasize organism–environment coupling, mutuality, self-organization, and change within nested systems over time. Developmental ecology has also been used more narrowly for the proximal environments in which development occurs.

These approaches provide important precedents rather than a ready-made theory for HAIDR.

HAIDR does not use developmental ecology as a synonym for any one of these theories, nor simply to mean “the user’s context.” It adopts the term as a candidate integrative construct because three ecological commitments are especially important for human–AI research:

development is relational; configuration matters; and history matters.

A human–AI developmental ecology is the structured configuration within which repeated participation occurs: human capacities and intentions; model characteristics; interaction protocol; memory and continuity; scaffolding; tools; interface and modality; social and material context; and prior participation history.

These components jointly organize who or what performs which cognitive operations, under what conditions, repeatedly, across time.

That shifts the unit of analysis. Rather than asking only: What does access to AI do to performance?, HAIDR asks: “How does changing the configuration of a human–AI developmental ecology change the forms of participation repeatedly enacted by the human — and what developmental consequences follow?”

The human at time t + 1 need not be developmentally identical to the human who entered at time t. Participation history can therefore become part of the causal architecture of subsequent participation.

This is the principal reason developmental ecology is currently the strongest candidate term: it keeps the research object larger than a model, prompt, interface, or individual interaction while making the environment available for systematic description and experimental manipulation.

3. The empirical move: locating development

Suppose two people complete the same task. One works independently; another works with an AI system and performs substantially better.

That establishes an effect on supported performance; it does not yet establish an effect on human development.

HAIDR therefore treats performance inside the coupled system and persistent change in the human as analytically distinct outcomes.

The central scientific question is:

What configurations of human–AI participation cultivate durable human capacities — and how can we know when development has actually occurred?

The empirical strategy is to manipulate features of the developmental ecology while holding tasks or systems sufficiently constant, then test what becomes independently available to the human.

Relevant evidence includes persistence over time, transfer to novel contexts, spontaneous reuse, delayed performance, response to perturbation, progressive scaffold reduction, and performance following AI withdrawal.

HAIDR does not assume that AI assistance either develops or degrades human capacity. Different configurations may produce amplification, scaffolding, substitution, displacement, or combinations that change across time and developmental stage.

The scientific problem is to distinguish them.

4. Current empirical work: Study 0

HAIDR is being developed alongside an initial empirical program rather than solely as a theoretical proposal.

Study 0 is an intensive longitudinal, theory-generating case study built around an unusually extensive archive of sustained human–AI research interaction. It reconstructs candidate developmental changes and processes capable of generating stronger prospective hypotheses.

The retrospective component examines longitudinal evidence for changes in conceptual discrimination, metacognition, integration, criticism, synthesis, spontaneous reuse, and cross-context transfer. Microgenetic analyses trace episodes in which a distinction appears, becomes recognized and reformulated, affects subsequent activity, and is later reused.

A prospective component then introduces structured research-reasoning probes, delayed transfer, perturbation, and scaffold withdrawal.

The central inferential problem is deliberately conservative:

Which observed changes provide evidence of development in the human participant, which remain dependent on the coupled human–AI system, and what evidence could distinguish the two?

Study 0 is intended to generate mechanisms, measurement problems, candidate constructs, and experimentally tractable hypotheses — not population-level effects from a single intensive case.

5. From longitudinal observation to experimental developmental science

The next phase translates those hypotheses into controlled experimental environments.

The basic experimental logic is:

configure ecology
→ organize participation
→ observe repeated cognitive enactment
→ measure change longitudinally
→ reduce or alter scaffolding
→ test persistence and transfer

Several prospective projects instantiate different parts of this program. The Wunderhorn Project develops parallel laboratories in music decomposition/recomposition and human development, using the highly specified structure of musical learning to identify candidate mechanisms for experimentally controlled studies of developmental change. AI-Mediated Research Methodology Acquisition tests whether changing AI scaffolding can accelerate research apprenticeship while producing independently usable methodological reasoning rather than scaffold-dependent performance. A Developmental Dashboard explores longitudinal measurement and feedback architectures, while Wayfinder extends the framework toward adaptive environments that vary challenge, friction, scaffolding, and scaffold reduction over time.

Together these projects point toward the longer-range ambition of HAIDR:

a design science of human–AI developmental ecologies.

The objective is not merely to document whether AI use has beneficial or harmful developmental effects, but to establish increasingly reliable principles for constructing human–AI environments that cultivate particular human capacities.
