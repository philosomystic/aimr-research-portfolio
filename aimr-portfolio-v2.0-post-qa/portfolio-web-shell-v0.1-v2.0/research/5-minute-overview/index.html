Human–AI Developmental Research

How do sustained forms of human–AI participation shape what people, organizations, and societies become capable of?

5-minute overview · canonical portfolio version · September 2026

Artificial intelligence is arriving amid a period of unusually dense and interacting global challenges: ecological disruption, geopolitical instability, institutional fragility, economic transformation, information disorder, technological acceleration, and persistent failures of collective coordination.

These problems differ in their causes, but together they pose a recurring question:

How can human beings and human systems become more capable of responding wisely to the conditions they are creating?

AI matters to this question because it is becoming part of the environments through which people think, learn, decide, relate, organize, create knowledge, and exercise agency. It is not simply another technological tool or another proposal for improving human behavior. It increasingly participates in cognition, education, science, work, institutions, governance, culture, and collective problem-solving.

In that sense, AI can be understood not only as a new technological capability but as a historically consequential intervention into human and collective capability.

The Human–AI Developmental Research program (HAIDR) asks what follows when those interactions become persistent.

The problem is no longer simply what AI can do

Human–AI research has already moved well beyond model benchmarks. Researchers study human–AI teaming, cognitive offloading, learning and skill, delegation, organizational adoption, agentic oversight, collective effects, and post-deployment behavior.

The unresolved problem is increasingly one of integration and inference across configurations, levels, and time.

Recent work already recognizes interaction design, changing human–machine relationships, organizational and societal implications, multilevel effects, and the importance of longitudinal and context-sensitive research. At the same time, the literature continues to describe fragmentation across disciplinary and analytical levels, while post-deployment measurement of human–AI feedback loops and beneficial human outcomes remains comparatively immature.

We therefore increasingly know that configuration matters. But we know much less reliably:

Which configurations produce which durable consequences, under what conditions, through which mechanisms, and with what degree of transportability?

  • Improved task performance does not establish increased human competence.
  • Higher individual productivity does not establish greater collective intelligence.
  • Organizational throughput does not establish preserved expertise or resilience.
  • Formal human accountability does not establish practical capacity to intervene.
  • Successful applications of AI to particular problems do not by themselves establish greater societal adaptive capacity.

The relevant scientific object is therefore increasingly not just the model, but the human–AI system embedded in its social and institutional environment.

HAIDR’s integrative move

Human–AI research already contains substantial work on learning, cognitive offloading, function allocation, interaction design, agency, organizational change, collective intelligence, and longitudinal effects.

HAIDR does not begin from the claim that these dimensions have been overlooked.

Its distinguishing move is to treat them as parts of a common developmental mapping problem:

How do nested conditions, distributions of human and artificial activity, and histories of repeated participation interact to produce different capability trajectories across people, coupled systems, collectives, and institutions?

The aim is therefore not simply to assemble multiple literatures under a broader vocabulary. It is to make their relations and inferential gaps explicit.

What evidence allows us to move from a particular human–AI configuration to claims about later human capability, the capability of the coupled human–AI system, collective knowledge and coordination, organizational expertise, institutional dependence, or resilience under changed conditions?

In compact form, HAIDR investigates the relation:

nested ecology → configuration → distribution of consequential operations → recurrent participation → developmental mechanisms → multilevel outcomes → longitudinal trajectory

Its proposed contribution depends on whether making those relations explicit yields better discrimination, explanation, measurement, and eventually design than studying the component pieces separately.

Human–AI developmental ecologies

HAIDR uses the term human–AI developmental ecology for the structured conditions through which recurrent human–AI participation occurs and through which its developmental consequences are produced.

The local interaction matters: model capability · interface · memory · continuity · initiative · delegation · authority · participation protocol · human capacities.

But the interaction is not a closed human–AI dyad. It occurs inside nested environments:

task → social / organizational → institutional → societal / political-economic → material / ecological

Those environments affect which patterns of human–AI participation become possible, rewarded, normalized, or difficult to resist. The same AI capability may therefore participate in very different developmental arrangements depending on the surrounding task, organizational incentives, institutional structure, and history.

The developmental question is therefore not simply: What does this AI system do to people?

What nested developmental ecology does a person or collective repeatedly participate in, and what trajectories does that ecology tend to produce?

From present performance to sustained capability

This is the recurring evaluative problem across HAIDR.

Two human–AI arrangements can produce comparable immediate results while generating very different futures.

  • One may strengthen independent judgment.
  • Another may support stable complementary specialization.
  • Another may remove opportunities through which expertise would otherwise develop.
  • Another may weaken lower-level fluency while strengthening higher-order supervisory capacity.
  • Another may increase organizational productivity while increasing dependence on a particular model, provider, or infrastructure.

For this reason, labels such as augmentation and replacement are often too coarse.

What capability changed, where does it now reside, what does it depend upon, and what happens when conditions change?

This requires distinguishing outcomes such as present performance · supported human–AI capability · retained human competence · intervention capability · higher-order orchestration · collective expertise · organizational capability · resilience · dependency.

Unaided human performance is not automatically the ideal. External support can constitute genuine capability. Some operations may reasonably migrate to artificial systems. Delegating lower-level activity may permit humans to develop valuable higher-order capacities.

The scientific problem is to distinguish among human development, productive specialization, stable dependence, capability displacement, and higher-order reorganization of agency.

Participation history

One way to investigate these trajectories is to examine the actual distribution of consequential operations over time.

“Human review,” for example, can describe very different arrangements:

  • a human makes an independent judgment before seeing AI advice;
  • the human compares an existing judgment with an AI recommendation;
  • the human performs targeted anomaly detection;
  • the AI performs the substantive reasoning while the human formally approves it.

Those workflows may look administratively similar while providing very different opportunities to practice judgment, detect error, revise, and learn.

HAIDR therefore investigates whether it is useful to represent participation through variables such as:

operation × actor × initiative × authority × sequence × timing of assistance × information provenance × feedback × opportunity for revision × recurrence

This is not proposed as a replacement for established work on function allocation, automation, mixed initiative, or joint human–AI activity. It earns a place only if a longitudinal record of participation improves discrimination among apparently similar systems, predicts later outcomes, supports better interventions, or yields more transportable design claims.

A multiscale research architecture

HAIDR connects three dimensions.

Nested ecological level — Where is the interaction occurring, and what larger systems organize it?

Developmental process — How does configuration shape the distribution of operations, recurrent participation, adaptation, and outcomes?

Timescale — What changes within an interaction, across repeated interactions, and across longer periods?

The developmental process can be represented schematically as:

configuration → distribution of consequential operations → recurrent participation → developmental mechanisms → multilevel outcome vector → trajectory

But the relation is recursive. Changed capacities alter later participation. Changed participation alters workflows and roles. Those arrangements may become institutionalized, and institutionalized arrangements reshape subsequent human–AI ecologies.

HAIDR also distinguishes three overlapping timescales:

Microgenetic — change occurring within particular episodes of participation.

Mesogenetic — patterns stabilizing, transferring, reorganizing, or becoming dependent across repeated episodes.

Longitudinal / ontogenetic — more durable shifts in repertoire, agency, expertise, dependence, identity, role, and realizable participation.

Research sites and current work

HAIDR is too broad to operationalize in a single study. Its value therefore depends on bounded research sites through which different parts of the architecture can be investigated.

Current research sites include AI-Mediated Reflection, anchored empirically by Study 0; organizational systems; neuroscience and passive sensing; collective intelligence; and Wunderhorn. The broader program also includes prospective work on developmental measurement and inference, AI-mediated research-methodology acquisition, and adaptive developmental interventions.

AI-Mediated Reflection

What forms of AI participation help a person become a better reflector rather than merely giving the person better reflections?

The research object is therefore not simply the quality of AI-generated reflection. It is the participation protocol: who formulates, interprets, challenges, differentiates, recognizes, revises, and eventually becomes able to perform those operations under changed or reduced support.

Study 0

Study 0 is the current empirical anchor within AIMR. It is an intensive longitudinal case of sustained human–AI reflective participation. Its retrospective phase reconstructs participation history and candidate developmental changes from an existing archive; its prospective phase uses repeated reasoning probes, delayed transfer, perturbation, and scaffold withdrawal to distinguish supported performance from persistent human capacity.

Study 0 is hypothesis-generating rather than evidence sufficient to validate HAIDR as a whole.

Organizational systems

Organizations provide a site for examining how AI changes the distribution of work, expertise, apprenticeship, intervention capacity, and practical control. A workflow may improve immediate throughput while reducing opportunities to develop the expertise later required for supervision, correction, mentoring, or recovery. Alternatively, delegation may enable new higher-order human capacities.

How do organizational and institutional arrangements stabilize particular distributions of human and artificial activity, and what capability trajectories result?

Neuroscience and passive sensing

Neuroscience and longitudinal sensing provide a possible source of mechanistic triangulation.

Does repeated participation in different human–AI ecologies alter the neural organization by which a person subsequently performs cognitive operations?

The strongest inference would combine participation history, longitudinal behavioral traces, transfer, perturbation or AI withdrawal, passive sensing, and neural evidence rather than treating any single measure as proof of development.

Collective intelligence

The developmental question scales beyond the individual. A human–AI arrangement may improve individual performance while altering epistemic diversity, correlated error, distributed expertise, information aggregation, or organizational resilience.

What is this group or institution becoming capable of, and what forms of independent expertise, correction, and adaptive capacity remain distributed within it?

Wunderhorn

Wunderhorn occupies a different place in the program. It uses musical decomposition, controlled transformation, and recomposition as a tractable domain for identifying candidate generative principles of organized development.

Those principles are not treated as evidence about human development. The epistemic boundary is explicit:

Analogy generates hypotheses; it does not establish mechanisms.

Candidate principles derived in music must be independently operationalized and tested elsewhere.

Wunderhorn Project — music as a laboratory for development

Toward a design science

The longer-term ambition is not to identify one universally ideal relationship between humans and AI. It is to develop conditional design knowledge.

Eventually, a mature design science would seek propositions of approximately this form:

Given a particular population, task, developmental objective, AI system, and surrounding ecology, which configuration of participation tends to produce one trajectory rather than another?

That is more demanding than general prescriptions such as keep a human in the loop, preserve productive friction, or augment rather than replace.

A meaningful design proposition must specify: for whom · doing what · under which conditions · with which distribution of operations · over what timescale · with what tradeoffs.

Which configurations produce which trajectories, for whom, under what conditions, across which levels and timescales?

HAIDR is not proposed as a comprehensive theory of human–AI development. Its value depends on whether connecting nested ecology, participation history, developmental timescale, and sustained capability produces better explanations, measurements, comparisons, and design decisions than existing approaches alone.

If it does not, the architecture should be revised or discarded.

The wider horizon

The empirical work begins with bounded human–AI systems. The wider concern is collective adaptive capacity.

AI is becoming part of the infrastructure through which societies conduct science, education, administration, economic activity, deliberation, forecasting, communication, and coordination.

It may strengthen capacities required to address complex challenges. It may also generate new forms of dependence, concentration, epistemic homogenization, inequality, and systemic vulnerability.

The broadest HAIDR question is therefore:

Under what conditions does increasing artificial intelligence actually increase humanity’s capacity to understand, govern, and respond wisely to the conditions it is creating?

That question is a horizon, not a conclusion. The nearer-term research task is more disciplined:

to establish better-warranted relations among ecology, configuration, participation, capability, and trajectory.
