Research program

AI-Mediated Reflection (AIMR)

A developing design science of developmental ecologies, currently focused on developmental change through sustained recursive human–AI participation.

1. The research problem

Most research on human–AI interaction asks what people can accomplish with AI, how users experience particular systems, or how particular interfaces affect immediate outcomes.

AI-Mediated Reflection (AIMR) asks a different question: what happens to the human over time through sustained participation with AI?

The central concern is whether repeated interaction merely improves performance while support is present, or contributes to durable changes in human capacities: how people perceive, discriminate, integrate, regulate, judge, choose, and act.

AIMR’s governing empirical question is:

What developmental changes occur through sustained recursive human–AI participation, through what mechanisms, under what ecological conditions, and how should those environments be structured?

This shifts the object of inquiry from isolated interactions toward time-extended developmental trajectories.

2. Developmental framing

AIMR treats sustained human–AI interaction as a developmental ecology: a configurable environment in which human capacities and intentions interact recursively with model characteristics, continuity, memory, interaction protocols, procedural scaffolding, tools, interfaces, social-material conditions, and the accumulated history of prior interaction.

The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not simply the human or the model in isolation. It is the coupled human–AI activity system and the developmental trajectory produced through repeated participation within it.

Within this ecology, change can occur at multiple timescales:

  • Microgenetic — changes unfolding within and across particular interactions;
  • Mesogenetic — integration, stabilization, or reorganization across days, weeks, and months;
  • Ontogenetic — more durable changes in capacities, dispositions, agency, and patterns of participation.

The central methodological problem is to determine when short-term changes accumulate into development—and when they do not.

A developmental ecology is a longitudinal feedback system in which recurring patterns of interaction alter the conditions of subsequent development.

participation → change in person and/or environment → changed conditions of subsequent participation → further change ↺

What is remembered, scaffolded, practiced, delegated, made salient, made easier, or made harder in one period can reorganize later participation. Development is therefore a trajectory produced through recursive changes in both person and environment.

AIMR System Architecture

Developmental ecology → recursive human–AI participation → microgenetic change → repeated participation across episodes → persistence/transfer testing → developmental trajectory → changed person and environment → reconfigured conditions of subsequent participation.

Across this trajectory, developmental coherence functions as an evaluative criterion: not whether a person becomes merely more internally consistent, but whether emerging organization remains corrigible by contact with consequential lived experience while remaining capable of further differentiation and transformation.

3. The crucial empirical distinction

Supported performance ≠ development

AI can make a person more capable while the system is present without producing any durable change in the person.

AIMR therefore distinguishes augmentation from development.

Improved performance during AI-supported interaction is not sufficient evidence that a human capacity has developed. Developmental claims require evidence that changes persist, generalize, and remain available when the conditions that originally supported them are altered.

AIMR therefore tests for:

Persistence

Does the change remain after a meaningful delay?

Transfer

Does it generalize to novel problems, domains, or contexts?

Scaffold reduction or withdrawal

What remains when AI assistance is reduced or removed?

Perturbation

Does the capacity remain available under changed conditions or competing demands?

Spontaneous reuse

Does the person independently re-enact distinctions, strategies, or practices originally supported during interaction?

Independence

What can the person now perceive, discriminate, organize, judge, or enact without relying on the original scaffold?

This gives AIMR an explicit falsifiability criterion:

If repeated controlled studies showed that sustained AI interaction reliably improves in-session performance but produces no persistent, transferable changes in human capacities after scaffold withdrawal, then AIMR’s claim that these systems can function as developmental ecologies would need substantial revision.

The distinction is consequential because an interaction can be highly productive without being developmental—and some forms of apparent enhancement may increase dependence rather than independent capacity.

4. What AIMR is trying to explain

AIMR proposes several interrelated processes through which sustained participation may produce developmental change.

Recursive participation

Development does not arise from exposure to model outputs alone. It emerges, when it emerges, through repeated cycles of participation in which perception, articulation, response, recognition, revision, and reuse reorganize subsequent interaction.

The quality of cognition therefore depends partly on the quality and structure of participation.

Differentiation

Differentiation is increased discriminatory resolution: the capacity to perceive distinctions that were previously unavailable, conflated, vague, or inaccessible.

A person may become better able to distinguish competing explanations, emotional states, conceptual categories, values, causal mechanisms, or alternative courses of action.

Integration

Integration concerns the organization and reorganization of relations among distinctions.

Development is not simply accumulating more distinctions. Newly differentiated material must alter the structure through which subsequent perception, reasoning, and action occur.

Coherence

Coherence evaluates the quality of that organization.

For AIMR, developmental coherence cannot mean mere consistency or stability. Coherence must remain corrigible by contact: capable of being revised when experience, evidence, consequences, or other perspectives reveal inadequacy.

Agency

Agency concerns increasingly self-authored and selectively enacted capacity: the ability to perceive possibilities, exercise judgment, choose among them, initiate action, monitor consequences, and correct course.

AIMR therefore asks not simply whether AI makes action easier, but whether repeated participation changes where initiative, judgment, and responsibility are located within the human–AI system.

Internalization and transfer

Internalization is not assumed from improved interaction performance. It is an empirical status demonstrated when previously scaffolded capacities become increasingly available independently.

Persistence, transfer, spontaneous reuse, and scaffold withdrawal therefore provide evidence about whether a developmental change has become available to the human—or remains dependent on the coupled system.

Additional AIMR constructs

The broader framework includes:

Developmental continuity — how accumulated interaction history alters subsequent participation.

Participation topology — how cognition, initiative, judgment, agency, responsibility, and learning are distributed across the coupled human–AI system.

Embodiment and world contact — how device, modality, posture, setting, mobility, relationships, material conditions, and access to the non-mediated world shape both interaction and development.

Temporal initiative — how responsibility for initiating, sustaining, redirecting, monitoring, and completing activity is distributed over time.

Developmental ecology — the configurable set of conditions through which different developmental trajectories become more or less likely.

5. From configured ecology to developmental trajectory

AIMR distinguishes several levels of the empirical problem. Features of the developmental ecology can be configured experimentally; those configurations influence participation and augmentation processes; those processes may alter candidate human capacities; and apparent changes must then be tested for where they reside, whether they persist and transfer, and what developmental trajectory they produce.

AIMR is developing not only a theory of developmental change, but a candidate architecture for specifying and testing what kinds of capacities are amplified, internalized, distributed, displaced, or reorganized through sustained human–AI participation.

Capacity distribution and developmental freedom

For any candidate capacity, AIMR asks how functional capacity is distributed across person, AI, and environment, and how that distribution changes over time: human-internalized ↔ coupling-dependent ↔ emergent co-intelligent, with mixed and dynamically changing configurations possible.

How should capacity be distributed across person, AI, and environment so that the overall ecology increases human developmental freedom without creating unacceptable displacement, dependency, or loss of contact?

Developmental freedom does not require maximal independence from technology. It requires meaningful capacity to configure, understand, evaluate, revise, reduce, withdraw from, or deliberately sustain the coupling.

The design cycle is longitudinal rather than terminal: measurement informs redesign; redesign changes subsequent participation; and changed participation alters the conditions of later development.

6. What can actually be manipulated?

AIMR is not only a theory of development. It is a design science of developmental ecologies.

Different human–AI environments can be systematically configured and compared.

AIMR Configuration Model

Model × Continuity × Memory × Interaction Protocol × Tools × Interface / Embodied Context × Task

These are developmental-ecology configuration variables: upstream features that can be systematically manipulated or characterized to test how different ecologies alter participation, augmentation, capacity formation, and developmental trajectory.

Model

Capabilities, reliability, interaction style, challenge, transparency, responsiveness, and other characteristics of the artificial participant.

Continuity

Whether interaction persists across episodes, how stable the relationship is, and whether accumulated history meaningfully structures subsequent participation.

Memory

What information is retained, accessible, synthesized, and reintroduced across interactions.

Interaction protocol

Who initiates, asks, challenges, interprets, evaluates, redirects, and closes loops; how scaffolding and feedback are structured; and how much productive friction is preserved.

Tools

Search, retrieval, external representations, coding, data analysis, action systems, and other affordances that extend the coupled system.

Interface and embodied context

Device, modality, posture, mobility, setting, interruption structure, latency, representation persistence, environmental access, privacy, and proximity to consequential action.

Task

The goals, complexity, novelty, duration, evaluation demands, and cognitive or practical capacities required by the activity.

AIMR asks how different configurations change:

  • what cognitive work the human performs;
  • which capacities are repeatedly exercised;
  • where initiative and judgment are located;
  • what becomes cognitively offloaded;
  • what becomes scaffold-dependent;
  • what becomes independently available;
  • and how those patterns accumulate across time.

The central experimental principle is therefore:

Hold the task and relevant system properties constant, manipulate features of the developmental ecology, and test what persists afterward.

7. Current empirical program

AIMR is developing through a sequence of theory generation, empirical probing, controlled experimentation, and longitudinal study.

The methodological rhythm is deliberately iterative:

conceptual differentiation → exploratory observation → operationalization → small empirical probe → construct revision → another probe → stabilization → stronger intervention → longitudinal study

rather than:

interesting idea → build product

Technical systems are therefore subordinate to the epistemic objective: what must be instantiated in order to answer the theoretical or behavioral question?

Study 0 — Intensive longitudinal case

Study 0 is an intensive longitudinal, theory-generating investigation of a sustained human–AI interaction archive and its ongoing continuation.

Its function is not to establish causal claims from a single case. It is to:

reconstruct developmental patterns → formulate candidate constructs and mechanisms → derive operational hypotheses → design stronger tests elsewhere.

Its current empirical architecture is:

Study 0R → Study 0R-X → CDAVA-informed construct differentiation → Study 0P → comparative / experimental program

Study 0R — Retrospective Reconstruction traces candidate developmental trajectories across accumulated interaction history.

Study 0R-X — External Digital Trace Triangulation extends the retrospective reconstruction with external traces capable of corroborating, qualifying, or contradicting archive-based interpretations.

CDAVA-informed construct differentiation maps candidate changes against a broader developmental and augmentation architecture and established constructs.

Study 0P — Prospective Developmental Probes tests whether previously scaffolded capacities appear independently under delay, transfer, perturbation, and reduced-support conditions.

Experimental Program

The experimental program decomposes the developmental ecology into manipulable configuration variables.

Studies can systematically vary conditions such as:

  • participation structure;
  • model behavior;
  • challenge and productive friction;
  • continuity;
  • memory;
  • scaffolding;
  • initiative allocation;
  • interface and embodied context;
  • and scaffold fading or withdrawal.

The aim is not merely to identify which systems produce better immediate outcomes, but which configurations produce different developmental trajectories.

Longitudinal program

The stronger developmental claims require repeated measurement across meaningful periods of time.

Longitudinal studies will investigate whether changes:

  • stabilize or disappear;
  • transfer into novel contexts;
  • become independently available;
  • generalize into everyday behavior;
  • vary across individuals and developmental histories;
  • or alter the conditions of subsequent participation.

This includes a crucial ecological-validity question: does change observed within AI-mediated interaction become visible in ordinary life?

For example:

  • Does AI-supported agency transfer into more self-authored action?
  • Does increased reflective coherence change actual decision patterns?
  • Does AI-mediated emotional regulation generalize into interpersonal situations?
  • Does dependence on AI increase or decrease across ordinary contexts?
  • Do people spontaneously reuse distinctions or practices without the model present?
  • Does increased AI mediation alter contact with other people, environments, movement, or unmediated experience?

Development must ultimately remain corrigible not only by laboratory measures, but by consequential contact with the world in which the person actually lives.

8. Current empirical focus

Current empirical focus: distinguishing improved AI-supported performance from persistent, transferable changes in human capacities after scaffold reduction or withdrawal.

Several immediate questions follow:

When does recursive scaffolding become independently retained capacity?

If an AI repeatedly helps a person differentiate concepts, perspectives, or possibilities, do those discriminatory capacities subsequently appear on unassisted novel tasks?

Which developmental-ecology configurations increase development rather than dependence?

How do continuity, memory, initiative, challenge, and scaffolding affect whether human capacities are exercised, displaced, strengthened, or rendered increasingly scaffold-dependent?

How does the topology of participation affect what develops?

Amount of AI assistance is a poor descriptor of scholarly or cognitive agency. What matters is how cognition and agency are distributed across the system.

Which participant formulates the problem? Generates distinctions? Evaluates alternatives? Detects errors? Exercises judgment? Initiates correction? Integrates learning?

What forms of capacity distribution preserve developmental freedom?

When is coupling-dependent capacity a beneficial form of complementary offloading, and when does it become displacement or dependency? Which configurations preserve meaningful ability to evaluate, revise, reduce, withdraw from, or deliberately sustain the coupling?

Does AI-mediated change transfer into lived participation?

Do changes observed in reflective interaction alter subsequent decisions, relationships, regulation, behavior, environmental engagement, or independent action?

Together, these questions move AIMR from asking whether AI is “helpful” toward a harder question:

What kinds of humans and human capacities are particular forms of sustained human–AI participation helping to produce?

9. Position in the field

AIMR draws on multiple established research traditions, including:

developmental psychology · learning sciences · ecological and dynamical systems approaches · distributed and extended cognition · human–computer interaction · human–AI interaction · media psychology · cognitive science · philosophy of mind · theories of agency · developmental and longitudinal methodology

Its claim is not that these literatures have failed to study human change.

Rather, AIMR identifies an emerging object that increasingly requires them to be brought together:

the developmental consequences of sustained recursive participation with increasingly persistent, capable, and embedded artificial systems.

Existing research already examines AI-assisted performance, cognitive offloading, learning, behavioral outcomes, human–AI collaboration, wellbeing, agency, social relationships, scaffolding, and long-term interaction.

AIMR’s distinctive contribution is to organize these phenomena around developmental trajectory:

  • What changes?
  • Through what mechanism?
  • Under what ecology of participation?
  • At what timescale?
  • What persists?
  • What transfers?
  • What remains scaffold-dependent?
  • What becomes independently available?
  • How does the changed person subsequently participate differently?
  • And by what criteria should those trajectories be evaluated?
