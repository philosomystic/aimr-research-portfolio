Human–AI Scholarship Method

A concise framework for understanding how scholarly cognition and agency are distributed across a human–AI system.

Scholarly agency is a problem of participation topology

Generative AI has made conventional descriptions of scholarly assistance increasingly inadequate.

Statements such as “AI was used extensively,” “AI helped with writing,” or “the scholar used AI throughout the project” tell us relatively little about where the intellectual work actually occurred. Two research processes can involve similarly intensive AI use while differing radically in how cognition, initiative, judgment, responsibility, and learning are distributed.

Amount of AI assistance is a poor descriptor of scholarly agency. What matters is the topology of participation.

The central question is not simply:

How much AI was used?

It is:

How is scholarly cognition and agency distributed across the human–AI system?

What is being distributed?

Scholarship consists of many distinguishable cognitive and epistemic functions:

problem formation → conceptual exploration → search → retrieval → interpretation → differentiation → synthesis → criticism → judgment → writing → verification → revision → integration

These functions need not all be performed by the same participant.

In one human–AI workflow, the human may formulate the research problem, determine conceptual distinctions, assess evidence, challenge interpretations, synthesize competing accounts, and make final judgments, while AI accelerates retrieval, comparison, organization, and articulation.

In another, the AI may formulate the problem, determine the conceptual structure, select evidence, generate interpretations, resolve tensions, and produce the final synthesis, while the human primarily approves or edits the resulting output.

Both workflows might reasonably be described as “AI-assisted scholarship.”

Developmentally and epistemically, however, they are not equivalent.

The relevant methodological object is therefore the distribution of scholarly functions across the coupled system:

  • Who initiates the inquiry?
  • Who determines what counts as a relevant distinction?
  • Who notices contradictions?
  • Who evaluates competing explanations?
  • Who decides whether evidence is sufficient?
  • Who synthesizes the final account?
  • Who verifies claims?
  • Who detects and corrects error?
  • Who retains the resulting conceptual organization afterward?

This distribution constitutes the topology of participation.

Three different things can be evaluated

A human–AI research process should not be evaluated only by the quality of its final artifact.

At least three analytically distinct outcomes matter.

1. Quality of the scholarly product

Is the resulting argument, analysis, literature synthesis, experiment, or written artifact accurate, coherent, original, well-supported, and useful?

This remains indispensable. Poor scholarship does not become valuable merely because the human performed more of it unaided.

2. Quality of participation

How were scholarly functions distributed while the work was being produced?

Did AI expand access to difficult cognitive work while preserving human discrimination and judgment? Did it create opportunities for criticism and revision? Or did it systematically substitute for the very processes through which scholarly understanding normally develops?

Two artifacts of comparable quality may emerge from very different participation structures.

3. Developmental consequences for the scholar

What happened to the human participant as a result of repeated involvement in the workflow?

Did the scholar become better able to:

  • formulate research problems;
  • discriminate among concepts;
  • interpret evidence;
  • detect weak arguments;
  • synthesize literatures;
  • articulate complex ideas;
  • challenge initial framings;
  • or exercise independent scholarly judgment?

Or did increasingly capable assistance allow those functions to migrate progressively out of the human participant?

This third question is specifically developmental.

Supported scholarship ≠ scholarly development

What the coupled human–AI system can produce is not by itself evidence of what the human has learned or become capable of doing.

A scholar may participate in the production of unusually sophisticated work while remaining unable to reproduce the relevant reasoning independently.

For that reason, claims about scholarly development require evidence beyond successful AI-supported performance.

Stronger evidence includes:

Independent reconstruction

Can the scholar reconstruct the central argument or conceptual structure without the system?

Delayed transfer

Does the capacity remain available after time has passed?

Novel application

Can the scholar apply the distinction, method, or reasoning structure to a different problem?

Spontaneous reuse

Does the scholar independently reintroduce previously scaffolded concepts or strategies when they become relevant?

Criticism without AI

Can the scholar identify weaknesses, alternatives, and counterarguments without external prompting?

Synthesis under scaffold withdrawal

Can the scholar organize complex material when the AI's contribution is reduced or removed?

These tests distinguish jointly supported scholarly performance from persistent changes in human scholarly capacity.

The objective is not minimal AI use

This framework does not assume that more human labor is intrinsically better, nor that scholarship becomes more authentic as AI assistance approaches zero.

Some forms of cognitive friction are unnecessary.

Search, retrieval, formatting, transcription, comparison, restructuring, and other activities can impose costs that contribute little to learning or judgment. AI may reduce those costs and allow scholars to participate in more demanding forms of inquiry.

The relevant question is therefore not:

How do we preserve as much unaided work as possible?

It is:

Which forms of cognitive work are developmentally generative, and which can be delegated without displacing the capacities scholarship is intended to cultivate?

This makes the design problem substantially more precise.

AI can function as:

  • an external memory system;
  • a retrieval and comparison layer;
  • a conceptual interlocutor;
  • an adversarial critic;
  • a scaffold for articulation;
  • a synthesis partner;
  • a methodological tutor;
  • or an increasingly autonomous intellectual delegate.

Each arrangement produces a different topology of participation.

The empirical question is how those arrangements affect both scholarly output and scholarly development.

From workflow description to research method

A mature human–AI scholarship methodology would therefore document not simply whether AI was used, but how major scholarly functions were distributed across the research process.

The relevant unit of analysis might include:

human initiative · AI initiative · delegation · verification · correction · judgment · scaffold dependence · retained capacity

This makes it possible to compare workflows that otherwise appear superficially identical.

It also creates a bridge to experimental research. Holding the scholarly task relatively constant, researchers could manipulate participation structures—for example:

  • human-first versus AI-first problem formulation;
  • direct answers versus guided questioning;
  • AI-generated synthesis versus human-generated synthesis;
  • persistent versus fading scaffolds;
  • AI criticism versus self-criticism;
  • or full access versus delayed scaffold withdrawal.

Subsequent independent performance could then test which structures improve both immediate scholarship and retained human capacity.

Practical implication

The aim is not to minimize AI participation.

It is to design forms of AI-mediated scholarship in which technical assistance remains subordinate to epistemic responsibility and in which the topology of participation supports rather than displaces the capacities scholarship is supposed to cultivate.

This reframes the central methodological question:

Not: How much of the scholarship did AI do?

but:

Which scholarly functions were performed by whom, how did that distribution change over time, and what became more independently available to the human as a result?

That is the level at which questions of scholarly agency, learning, responsibility, and development become empirically tractable.
