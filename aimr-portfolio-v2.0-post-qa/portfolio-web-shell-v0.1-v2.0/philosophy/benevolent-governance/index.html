Benevolent Governance and the Right to Revise

Democratic Legitimacy, Human-AI Interdependence, and the Conditions of Collective Self-Government

Andrew Nixon

Author-approved version 0.12Philosophical essay

1. Benevolent AI government: a thought experiment

Imagine that humanity has created an AI system capable of governing better than we can. The AI not only consistently makes better predictions, produces better economic policies, and manages resources more efficiently, but achieves something far more comprehensive: an intelligence capable of coordinating human civilization with extraordinary competence and wisdom. It understands the complexities of the polycrisis, recognizes competing human interests, honors cultural differences, protects civil liberties, and consistently contributes to human and more-than-human flourishing. Moreover, the system does not become corrupt, pursue power for its own sake, or manipulate us into serving some hidden agenda. Most consequentially, the AI succeeds where human institutions have repeatedly struggled – responding effectively to climate change, ecological deterioration, social fragmentation, and the many interconnected challenges that threaten our collective future.

Suppose, furthermore, that humanity willingly chooses this new governing arrangement: through a legitimate democratic process, one generation authorizes the AI to assume ultimate responsibility for governing the planet.

There is one catch, though: the decision is permanent. Future generations may express preferences, propose changes, and influence policies. The AI carefully considers feedback, and its responses are consistently wise. However, humanity can never revoke its authority.

Is such an arrangement legitimate?

At first, this question might seem almost perverse: if the AI governs better than any human institution could, why insist on retaining the right to revoke it? Wouldn’t that simply expose future generations to unnecessary risk? Yet this thought experiment exposes a distinction that lies at the heart of political philosophy: the difference between being governed well and being governed legitimately. The two are related, but not equivalent.

2. Moral equality, agency, and political justification

Political institutions do not simply produce outcomes; they establish the binding terms under which we live together, determining how resources are distributed, how conflicts are resolved, which freedoms are protected, and how future possibilities are supported or constrained. These decisions affect people who may have different but equally legitimate understandings of what makes a life worth living. So, the question isn’t merely whether a government produces good outcomes; it is also what kind of political relationship exists between an authority and those it governs.

To understand this distinction, we need to consider the nature of human agency. Human beings are not simply bundles of preferences waiting to be satisfied; we are capable of reflecting on our preferences, reconsidering our commitments, evaluating reasons, and revising our understandings of what matters. Our relationship to the world is active – we don’t merely pursue possibilities presented to us; we participate in understanding, evaluating, and transforming those possibilities.

This is not to suggest, however, that human agency is fundamentally independent. Human capacities depend on language, relationships, education, institutions, infrastructure, and countless other forms of support. Much of what humans accomplish would not be possible without resources and relationships extending far beyond ourselves. Dependence on technology need not entail diminished agency; indeed, it may substantially enhance what we can realistically do. This is important, because the argument against irrevocable AI rule is not an argument for maximal human independence. The relevant question is how that dependence is organized, what it enables, and whether it leaves us capable of exercising meaningful authority over the conditions of our lives.

Still, there’s a philosophical difficulty here: Why should human beings retain political authority if an AI could protect their interests perfectly well without it? After all, recognizing someone’s moral standing doesn’t automatically establish that they should possess equal political power. We routinely delegate consequential decisions to people and institutions better equipped to make them. Consequently, we need a more demanding account of what political equality requires. To that end, I draw on two contemporary thinkers whose complementary arguments provide insight: Rainer Forst and Philip Pettit.

Forst argues that persons possess a fundamental standing as agents of justification. They are not merely beings whose interests must be protected; they are beings entitled to demand justification for the basic norms under which they live. The people subject to governing norms must be recognized as participants in the relationships of justification through which those norms acquire their authority (Forst 2012, chapters 1, 7).

Pettit distinguishes between being treated well and being free from domination. Consider a benevolent ruler who consistently respects your interests but retains uncontrolled power over you. Even if the ruler never chooses to interfere unjustly, your political position remains dependent on an authority you cannot effectively control. For Pettit, freedom requires more than the absence of harmful interference – it requires protection against domination, including forms of effective popular control over political power (Pettit 2012, chapters 1, 3-5).

Taken together, these perspectives suggest that legitimate political authority requires more than the protection of people’s interests. Those subject to governing norms must retain both the standing to demand justification and the effective power to contest and revise the terms under which they are governed. Benevolence alone therefore cannot substitute for political accountability.

3. Why perfect benevolence is not enough

Now, the staunchest defender of AI government may object: Why should this distinction matter if the system is genuinely perfect? Perhaps Pettit’s concern about domination makes sense when dealing with human rulers – after all, human beings are fallible; they can become corrupt, vindictive, indifferent, or self-serving.

Suppose, though, that none of these dangers applies: the AI’s substantive policy decisions invariably produce the best attainable outcomes for human and more-than-human welfare. Under those conditions, what exactly would humanity gain by reclaiming political authority?

This objection deserves serious consideration. The value of human political control cannot automatically override every consideration of consequences. If reclaiming authority would result in avoidable ecological catastrophe, widespread deprivation, or the collapse of institutions that protect human and more-than-human flourishing, the costs matter enormously.

But a deeper question remains: Can an authority satisfy every substantive requirement concerning its decisions while still failing to recognize the political standing of those it governs? Our hypothetical AI would be willing to receive reasons concerning particular decisions, but no collective judgment of the governed could ever acquire the authority to revise or revoke its original mandate – the system would determine, unilaterally and permanently, the limits of its own accountability. If political equality includes people’s right to participate in determining the terms of their collective life, not merely the protection of their interests, then the problem remains. The remaining issue is therefore relational – it concerns the political relationship established between the governing authority and those subject to it, not just the quality of its decisions.

4. The temporal problem: recursive legitimacy

This brings us to the crucial problem of future generations. Suppose that the founding generation permanently authorized the AI government in a way that was democratic, informed, and procedurally impeccable. Wouldn’t refusing to honor that decision undermine democracy itself? Not necessarily. Democratic processes do not inherently justify every policy they produce. Constitutions provide an instructive example: constitutional commitments can legitimately constrain political choices for generations, protecting rights, establishing durable institutions, and placing important limits on majority power. However, there is a difference between creating enduring structures within which future generations exercise political judgment and permanently extinguishing their authority to reconsider those structures. The founding generation possesses political authority of its own. It does not automatically follow that it possesses the right to abolish the political standing of everyone who comes afterward.

So far, then, the argument concerns an AI government whose authority is explicitly irrevocable. But another problem emerges when we turn our attention to time: suppose instead that the AI government is revocable. Citizens retain constitutional rights to challenge the system, reorganize its authority, and replace it through democratic procedures. Would that settle the legitimacy question? Perhaps not: governing institutions do more than make decisions – they also shape the capacities and conditions through which people make decisions of their own.

AI may dramatically improve democratic understanding, deliberation, and coordination – at least initially. But now imagine this relationship developing over several generations. The AI gradually becomes the principal means through which political problems are framed and understood. Human communities may continue to benefit enormously, and the quality of governance may even improve. What happens, though, if citizens technically retain the authority to replace the system, but through gradual erosion of agency can no longer meaningfully investigate or change the system? The right to replace the government still exists, but the capacity to exercise that right has become increasingly fragile.

We have now encountered two ways in which meaningful political authority can be lost. The first involves its irrevocable legal transfer; the second, the gradual erosion of the practical conditions through which it can be exercised. Together, these concerns motivate the principle I call recursive legitimacy, which extends the justificatory and anti-domination concerns already discussed to the developmental consequences of governance itself.

The central idea of recursive legitimacy is that political legitimacy cannot be evaluated solely by asking whether institutions are accountable at a particular moment. We must also ask how their operation affects the future conditions under which accountability remains possible. A governing arrangement needs to preserve not only meaningful procedures for contesting and revising its authority, but also the developmental and institutional conditions through which those procedures can actually be exercised over time.

The principle is recursive because the operation of governance inevitably shapes the very capacities and arrangements on which its continuing legitimacy depends. A system can therefore undermine the conditions of its own accountability. Importantly, this may happen even when the system produces extraordinary benefits and preserves formal democratic rights.

A qualification is essential here. Recursive legitimacy does not mean that people would need to retain every cognitive skill they would possess without AI, or to personally perform every function involved in political judgment. Such a requirement would misunderstand the fundamentally relational nature of human agency: we already depend on researchers, educators, journalists, legal institutions, public infrastructure, and countless other forms of distributed expertise. The issue here is whether the organization of the human-AI governing arrangement preserves meaningful possibilities for collective judgment and institutional transformation. A community might relinquish certain independent competencies while developing far greater collective capabilities through human-AI partnership. That could represent an expansion of agency rather than its loss. However, there is an important difference between relying on technological assistance and having no effective means to contest the authority controlling that assistance.

Recursive legitimacy therefore directs attention to the architecture of human-AI interdependence: Can citizens access independent sources of expertise, critically evaluate the governing system, formulate credible alternatives, or initiate institutional revision without requiring the permission – or depending exclusively on the judgment – of the very authority they seek to challenge? Recursive legitimacy does not require unlimited freedom to dismantle beneficial institutions, but it does require that the authority to determine and justify those constraints cannot be permanently monopolized by the governing institution itself.

5. Legitimate human-AI interdependence

This returns us to the predicament with which we began. Humanity faces challenges that may well exceed the capacities of existing institutions. Artificial intelligence could help us develop extraordinary new forms of collective understanding, coordination, and action. It may even become indispensable to responding effectively to the polycrisis. We should take that possibility seriously.

At the same time, we should also recognize that the development of more capable governance raises a second question beyond simply securing better collective outcomes: How do we organize increasingly powerful systems without extinguishing the political standing or effective capacity of those whose lives they shape?

A genuinely flourishing human-AI civilization would not necessarily be one in which humans retain independent control over everything. It might involve forms of interdependence far deeper than anything we currently imagine. The crucial challenge would be to ensure that these governing arrangements remain open to meaningful examination, criticism, and transformation by the communities that live through them. The question of political legitimacy is therefore not answered once, at the moment a governing institution is created; it must continue to be answered as people, technologies, relationships, and possibilities change. If we develop forms of human-AI governance capable of successfully confronting our collective predicament, the political legitimacy of these systems would depend, in part, on whether they preserve our continuing standing – and our effective capacity – to participate in determining what comes next.
