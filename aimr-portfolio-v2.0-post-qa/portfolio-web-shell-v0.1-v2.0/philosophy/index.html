Philosophy of Human–AI Futures

What becomes possible, impossible, accessible, or inaccessible—and for whom, through which arrangements, and with what significance?

Working philosophical programDoctoral research direction · Fall 2027

An intellectual orientation

My central philosophical concern is how increasingly capable artificial intelligence is transforming the conditions of human and collective possibility: what becomes possible, impossible, accessible, or inaccessible; for whom; through which arrangements; and with what implications for human flourishing.

My graduate studies in Philosophy, Cosmology, and Consciousness at the California Institute of Integral Studies cultivated an enduring interest in consciousness, human development, complex systems, and the larger conditions of meaningful existence. My current work brings this integrative orientation to an emerging philosophical problem: how humanity might understand and navigate its relationship with increasingly intelligent artificial systems.

I am particularly interested in rigorous normative frameworks that connect philosophy to empirical inquiry, institutional design, and public policy.

Central questions

Agency and development

When do sustained human–AI relationships expand human capabilities and future possibilities? When does dependence become disempowering?

Consciousness and moral standing

How should intelligence, functional agency, subjective experience, and moral considerability be distinguished as artificial systems advance?

Collective life and governance

How should authority, responsibility, and meaningful participation be distributed across human–AI institutions?

Flourishing and transformation

How do we evaluate emerging forms of life when our conceptions of the good and the conditions of their development may themselves change?

An emerging argument: recursive legitimacy

Even a genuinely benevolent advanced AI system does not automatically acquire legitimate authority over the shared conditions of human life. Morally significant agents hold claims to shape their lives within ecologically interdependent and evolving systems. Initial consent cannot permanently extinguish the legitimate claims of other people, future generations, or affected beings.

I am exploring recursive legitimacy as a candidate principle: extensive delegation to AI may be permissible, but the terms of collective governance should remain meaningfully contestable and revisable as values, agents, institutions, and future possibilities change.

This is a provisional philosophical position requiring comparison with existing accounts of democratic legitimacy, relational autonomy, non-domination, capability, and intergenerational justice.

Research relationship and doctoral aims

The Philosophy of Human–AI Futures is broader than any single empirical methodology. HAIDR provides an independent empirical counterpart that can support, qualify, or challenge philosophical assumptions about agency, development, and participation. Neither program should be conflated with the other.

My doctoral aim is to become an empirically accountable integrative philosopher of AI and human flourishing, able to construct rigorous normative frameworks while engaging interdisciplinary research and consequential public questions.
